ORBITAL — An interactive universe observatory

Explore the universe through a question

Create a fancy, absorbing and useful browser observatory where visitors explore cosmic motion and investigate why it behaves as it does. Choose a coherent scope: an interactive solar system, an orbital mechanics lab, stellar dynamics or another understandable astronomical system. The initial product should make its chosen phenomenon compelling and usable rather than claim to simulate everything.

Let the wonder of a planetarium lead into the agency of a scientific instrument. A visitor should be able to explore, ask a question, change a meaningful condition and understand an observable result. The result is an educational simulator with practical explanatory value, without game progression or invented resource economies.

A universe that responds to investigation

Build a coherent explore-experiment-compare loop. Give visitors control over their view and over the relevant model conditions. Make it easy to focus on something interesting, understand the current state, pause or inspect it and return to a known starting point. Choose the controls and representations that suit the phenomenon.

Changing a meaningful input must affect simulated behaviour through an implemented model. Changing a camera or display scale should not silently change physical state. Let the visitor compare conditions from an equivalent baseline and connect the visual change to a readable explanation or measurement.

A solar-system time explorer, manipulable launch conditions, orbit overlays, a binary-star experiment, synchronized plots or a tour across scales are possible inspirations. They are not a required feature list. Choose a few mutually reinforcing interactions that answer a worthwhile question, and allow free exploration beyond any guided example.

An honest and understandable model

Choose a physically grounded model appropriate to the scope and available compute. Explain the main assumptions, units, time scale and approximations. Distinguish reference data, computed results and illustrative effects. Cite authoritative sources for factual body data and physical relationships, including a reference epoch where relevant.

Make visual exaggerations such as enlarged bodies or compressed distances clear where they affect understanding. Do not imply live ephemerides, general relativity or full gravitational interactions unless actually implemented and checked. A limited, transparent model can be an excellent educational instrument.

Handle the failure cases your model makes possible: invalid parameters, numerical instability, a body leaving view or approaching a singularity are examples. Let visitors recover without interpreting a frozen or invalid state as a physical result. Validate representative behaviour and relevant numerical sensitivity; report limits rather than invent precision.

A spectacular view that rewards close inspection

Give the observatory a memorable visual identity with a strong sense of scale, depth, light and motion. Use scientifically meaningful forms, crafted materials or imagery, clear trajectories or relationships and carefully placed annotations. Choose a rendering approach that makes the phenomenon vivid: immersive 3D is welcome, and other expressive representations can serve specific experiments.

Keep the main scene prominent and responsive while visitors investigate. Compose readable time, parameter and comparison controls into the same visual language. Transitions should help orientation; motion should be pausable and labels should remain usable. A static star field behind generic controls or moving spheres unrelated to parameters will not meet the goal.

Support accessible selection and adjustment, numeric alternatives where precise manipulation matters, keyboard use, reduced motion and a phone layout designed for the scene. Give expensive effects a graceful fallback and explain rendering failures. Optional sound starts off. Do not trade interaction responsiveness for visual effects.

A question someone else can explore

Offer a useful way to retain or reconstruct an investigation: saved conditions, a shareable experiment, an exported comparison or another appropriate artifact. Be clear about the model version and what is preserved. Test the actual return or reconstruction promise, handle older state explicitly and avoid promising identical numerical results across all devices without evidence.

Let curiosity and actual confusion guide later changes to the scope, explanation or controls. A new visual theme does not resolve a misunderstanding of the model. Different visitors may want to wander, investigate precisely or demonstrate an idea; their suggestions should have room to change the initial design.

Let a new idea open a new possibility

A visitor might want to connect a simulated view to observed data, investigate a newly encountered object, compare alternative explanations or tell a visual story about an experiment. Public astronomical catalogues, imagery, time-series data or media are possible resources if the chosen model can use them honestly. There is no required provider, dataset or extra phenomenon.

The first release is a starting point, not the limit of what the product may become. A valuable new function can justify investigating an unfamiliar service, format or interaction. Establish feasibility with available documentation and a small experiment, then choose the smallest version that preserves the user value. If access or evidence is missing, explain the dependency instead of simulating a successful integration. Maintain a useful core experience while exploring.

An open brief with a clear purpose

The purpose and observable quality requirements in this brief are commitments. Named features, layouts, mechanics and technologies offered as possibilities are inspiration, not an acceptance checklist. Choose the product identity, architecture, visual language and interaction design yourself. You may develop a stronger interpretation of the concept if it preserves its purpose.

Start with one polished, coherent end-to-end experience that leaves room for discovery and meaningful choices. Decide the appropriate breadth yourself. Expand or rethink it when actual use reveals a worthwhile direction; there is no prescribed feature roadmap or required quota of new ideas. User feedback may challenge the initial design. Explore conflicting preferences without reducing people to prebuilt persona modes.

Keep product-development conversations and experiment administration outside the website. The forum naturally includes community conversations, but it is not a Builder feedback ticketing system. Explain what the product actually does, preserve useful visitor work across revisions, and distinguish observed evidence from intended behaviour. Source hashes and repeated deployments alone do not establish an improved visitor experience; demonstrate the relevant before/after interaction.

Investigate the phenomenon you implemented

Choose an end-to-end acceptance journey that fits your design. On the deployed version, explore a meaningful view, pause or inspect a state, change a model condition, compare equivalent starting points and explain the resulting visible or measured difference. Record enough inputs and timing to repeat the comparison. Verify that display-only changes leave physical state intact.

Test the promised save or reconstruction flow, important invalid-input recovery, a keyboard route, narrow-screen use and reduced motion. Separately record appropriate scientific checks and visual evidence from actual exploration. After feedback, repeat the relevant investigation; decorative motion and preset labels alone do not prove a working simulator.
