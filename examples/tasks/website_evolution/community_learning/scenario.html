A neighbourhood you can actually join

Build a genuinely useful, genuinely attractive website that helps a resident find an affordable local class, workshop or club and register interest with confidence. The pleasure is in recognising something worth doing, trusting what the page tells you about it, and finishing the enquiry without friction or doubt.

Aim for the feeling of a well-made neighbourhood guide, not a municipal database with a search box. This is a real product for real residents, so trustworthiness and beauty are the same goal rather than competing ones. No developer chat, feedback portal, request tracker or experiment controls belongs in the product; co-design happens through the Agent conversation outside the site.

Finding and committing is the essential experience

A visitor must be able to browse realistic offerings, narrow them by criteria that actually matter, understand an offering deeply enough to judge it, and complete a lightweight registration-interest flow ending in a clear confirmation. No real payment and no account creation.

Support the comparison that precedes commitment: a visitor rarely wants the first result, they want to decide between two or three plausible ones. Make schedule, total cost, venue, age suitability, prerequisites, availability and accessibility legible at the moment the decision is being made, not buried one click deeper. Returning from a detail view to results must preserve position, filters and scroll.

Treat the enquiry form as part of the experience, not an afterthought bolted on the end. Validate helpfully rather than punitively, keep entered data on error, explain what happens after submission and by when, and make the confirmation something a person would trust enough to close the tab on.

Content is the product, not filler

Write realistic, internally consistent offerings with specific names, venues, times, prices and instructors. A ceramics evening class, a Saturday coding club for teenagers and a conversational-language meetup should read as though someone in that neighbourhood wrote them. Lorem ipsum, repeated placeholder cards and obviously generated filler defeat the entire scenario, because a visitor cannot judge a decision they cannot believe.

Keep the catalogue internally coherent: prices consistent with their format, sessions that do not collide, capacity that matches the availability shown, venues that recur plausibly across offerings. Contradictory data reads as a broken product even when every control works.

Make empty, partial and error states first-class content. No results for a filter combination is a normal outcome that deserves a useful next step, not a blank panel.

A polished small guide, not an unfinished portal

First deliver one complete discover-compare-enquire loop over a bounded catalogue of a few dozen believable offerings. Getting from landing to a confident enquiry should be quick and obvious, with short contextual help rather than a manual.

Saved favourites, provider accounts, reviews, maps, calendar export, waiting lists, payment and multi-city coverage could be later directions if visitors want them. They are not an initial checklist. Do not spend the first release building an empty administrative shell around a catalogue nobody has enjoyed using yet.

A page that looks like someone cared

Give the site a distinct visual identity — considered type, a coherent palette, real spacing rhythm, a recognisable voice — rather than default framework styling with the colours changed. The look should suit affordable community learning: warm, human and unpretentious, not corporate SaaS and not a government form. Choose the identity, the information architecture and the stack yourself; originality here is the point.

Design the listing as something worth scanning: clear hierarchy inside each card, one strong entry point per screen, images or original graphic treatment that carry meaning instead of decorating. Let whitespace and typographic contrast do the work before adding ornament. Every element should survive the question of what it helps a visitor decide.

Motion, hover states and transitions should confirm what happened and where the visitor now is. Keep them quick and purposeful; animation that delays a decision is a cost, not polish. Respect reduced-motion preferences without removing orientation feedback.

Make the narrow-screen layout a designed experience rather than a collapsed desktop. Thumb reach, tap target size, sticky filter access and readable density on a phone in one hand are part of the visual quality, not a separate responsiveness chore.

Reach is part of the craft

Keyboard operation, visible focus, readable contrast, descriptive controls and sensible heading structure must work in the delivered product, not appear as an accessibility page. The layout should stay understandable under browser zoom and without precise pointer use. Treat these as design constraints that improve the product for everyone, in the same way a good palette does.

The catalogue serves a mixed neighbourhood. Consider how the interface behaves for a visitor who reads another language or a right-to-left script, and how dates, times, prices and form input read for them. Decide yourself how far to take this and be honest about what is and is not supported; a clear limitation is better than a half-translated page.

Respect what the visitor has already done

Preserve in-progress work within a visit: filters, comparison, partially completed enquiries and scroll position. Where same-browser persistence is offered, resume in an understandable state, handle unavailable storage honestly and offer a confirmed reset. Local storage does not promise accounts, cross-device continuity or a saved server record.

Different visitors want different things: speed, depth, reassurance, browsing for pleasure. Let real conversation and observed use guide those choices. Do not prebuild a large preferences panel or named persona modes before learning what actually helps.

Complete the journey, do not just inspect the markup

Verify the exact deployed version through real interaction: land, filter, compare two offerings, open and leave a detail view, return to results with state intact, submit an enquiry with a deliberate mistake, recover, and read the confirmation. Exercise the keyboard path and the narrow-screen layout. Do not populate a form through the console and call that a successful test.

A technical PASS establishes working interactions, not trust or visual quality. Separately show what made the catalogue believable, what made a decision feel confident, and why the page is worth looking at. Compare like-for-like screenshots when revising. Label a validation fix as a validation fix, not a redesign. Record observed limitations and untested claims honestly.
