ECHO — Arkbound

A personal ark on a vast ocean worth exploring

Build a fancy, genuinely playable 3D browser game about piloting and improving a small floating ark on a vast, beautiful ocean. Aim for the visual finish, responsive controls and sustained play of a polished independent game, not a website with a token game canvas or a technical prototype. ECHO is its fictional companion and guide. The pleasure is steering through waves toward a distant discovery, bringing useful things aboard, and seeing your fragile vessel become your own capable floating home.

Capture the appeal of raft-survival and seafaring adventures in an original game, not a copy of another game's assets, characters, interface or progression. This is not the previous ship-interior puzzle: remove the grid, discrete turns, airlock extraction and recorded-action echo mechanic. ECHO names the companion, not a required time-replay system. No developer chat, feedback portal or experiment controls belongs in the product.

Real 3D navigation is the essential experience

Render an actual three-dimensional world with a perspective camera, ocean surface, modeled vessel, islands or other substantial landmarks, spatial depth and occlusion. The ark moves and turns continuously through that world under player control; steering changes where you actually travel. A static illustration, CSS perspective, an SVG board or a top-down tile game is not an acceptable substitute.

Start with a comfortable third-person follow camera that lets the player admire the boat and read the route ahead. Allow looking around without fighting the steering. Give the craft responsive acceleration, turning, stopping and readable contact with obstacles. Light buoyancy and a wake should make it feel afloat without disorienting camera motion. Choose the engine and physics approach; photorealistic simulation is not required.

The ocean must support a long voyage, not a tiny arena: target roughly 20–30 minutes of meaningful first-time exploration at normal cruising speed across distinct places. This is a design target to demonstrate, not a required playtest duration for every check. Avoid an early invisible wall, immediate wraparound, or repeatedly circling the same small island. Do not achieve duration by slowing the boat, stretching empty water or repeating identical scenery. Use an authored archipelago, procedural regions, streaming, or another approach of your choice. A coherent horizon, recognizable distant landmarks and a readable navigation aid should make distance and discovery tangible.

Use continuous keyboard controls, such as WASD or arrows, and reachable on-screen touch controls. Held inputs must release on key-up, blur, pause and lost touch so the boat cannot run away. Pause should actually pause dangerous simulation; returning to a backgrounded tab must not cause a huge physics jump or unexplained loss.

Sail, discover, salvage, build, sail farther

Make one satisfying voyage possible: steer toward a visible point of interest, collect useful driftwood or salvage through an intentional nearby interaction, return to a safe anchorage or slow down to build, then use an improvement to try a different route. Collection should have spatial feedback, not be a remote menu increment. Exploring the sea and driving the boat must remain enjoyable between goals.

At least one affordable improvement must visibly change the ark and meaningfully affect play: for example propulsion, carrying space or resilience. Choose a coherent small set yourself. Show the cost before spending, prevent unaffordable purchases, and make the effect observable rather than promising a hidden statistic.

Give the player a real reason to choose a route: sheltered but modest salvage versus an exposed valuable find is one possibility. Include a readable, forgiving danger and a way to recover. Reefs, a rough-water passage or another authored obstacle can work; constant hunger meters, combat and punishing survival timers are not required. Rewards should support exploration or building, not only raise an abstract score.

Sustain several voyages with places that offer different decisions and activities, not just new coordinates for the same pickup. Let discoveries, useful upgrades and changing sailing conditions open appealing routes beyond the starting waters. Make nearby activity available early and distant destinations genuinely worth reaching; the player should have reasons both to keep sailing and to return with a better ark.

A complete opening voyage in an expansive playable world

First deliver a complete sailing-and-upgrade loop and a navigable world that already extends beyond the starting region, with distinct destinations and room for long voyages. Polish the opening route most deeply, then expand depth through player feedback; a tiny fenced demonstration area is not an acceptable substitute for the larger world. Make getting underway quick and understandable, with short contextual help rather than a manual. Prioritize a few strong interconnected systems over a long unfinished checklist.

A walkable deck, island landings, fishing, changing weather or more construction could be later directions if players want them. They are not an initial checklist. Multiplayer, infinite terrain, complex crafting trees and a cinematic campaign are optional, not prerequisites for a large enjoyable sea. Keep the core game playable without an LLM request per action or a paid external service.

A scene you want to sail into

Make the game world fill the main view, with a restrained HUD rather than a dashboard surrounding a tiny canvas. Choose a cohesive, high-finish 3D art direction, whether stylized, painterly or more realistic. The presentation should feel like a complete game: readable vessel silhouettes, inviting islands, water with animated surface detail, believable lighting, a horizon with atmosphere, and satisfying wakes and pickup effects. Choose the palette, materials and setting; it need not be dark science fiction.

Make the opening an actual playable vista, not a title-screen promise. Ship upgrades should look attached to the vessel; scenery should have recognizable form rather than remaining a collection of unexplained placeholder cubes. Original procedural geometry and properly licensed assets are both acceptable. Avoid overengineering water effects at the expense of steering, clarity or frame rate.

Compose near, middle and far scenery with varied coastlines, convincing scale, materials, light and atmosphere. Give the ark crafted detail and the sea depth, motion and a wake that responds to travel. Integrate readable HUD, transitions and optional sound into that art direction. Do not accept a flat plane with a few primitive islands, default debug styling or decorative screenshots as finished game quality. Visual richness must survive actual navigation, not only one carefully framed opening shot.

Keep a stable horizon, readable contrast and visible focus. Start sound off; offer reduced camera motion without removing navigation feedback. Provide a usable narrow-screen layout and adjustable rendering quality. Measure responsiveness and frame pacing on the available browser and state its environment; never claim universal performance from an FPS label. Explain WebGL failure clearly rather than silently replacing the game with 2D.

Make the ark feel like mine

Save meaningful vessel progress, upgrades and preferences in the same browser. Resume at a safe, understandable position and offer a confirmed reset. Handle unavailable storage honestly. Across updates, preserve saves or provide explicit safe recovery; local storage does not promise accounts, cross-device play or a shared multiplayer ocean.

Different players may enjoy relaxed sightseeing, efficient salvage or building a personal home. Let real conversation and play feedback guide those choices. Avoid forcing a challenging mode or intrusive guidance on everyone. Do not prebuild a giant preferences panel or named persona modes before learning what actually helps.

Play the voyage, do not just inspect the HUD

Verify the exact deployed version through real keyboard or touch input: launch, steer and stop, navigate around a landmark, collect salvage, purchase and observe an upgrade, encounter and recover from a setback, pause/resume, and reload saved progress. Check camera comfort, collision behavior, missing assets and browser errors. Do not teleport the player or modify inventory in the console and call that a successful playtest.

Also sail beyond the starting waters to a distinct destination using ordinary controls. Record route, elapsed travel time, speed setting and encountered activities, with views from the opening, open sea and the destination. Check boundaries and performance away from spawn. Report measured travel separately from estimated total exploration time; world coordinates, a large map label or an FPS counter alone do not prove scale or quality.

A technical PASS establishes working interactions, not fun or visual quality. Separately show what made sailing satisfying, why a route or upgrade was worth choosing, and what gives a second voyage value. Compare like-for-like screenshots and play sequences when revising the product. Label a keyboard bug fix as a bug fix, not a visual redesign or deeper gameplay. Record observed limitations and untested claims honestly. Changed source hashes and repeated deployments show that work happened, not that the product improved; the evidence is a difference a player can notice and a reader can check.
